# What I learned in Qualitative Research Methods

**Dr. Emma Dixon · Clemson University · Fall 2025**

My individual course project examined how two Tesla drivers made sense of communication, control and trust in consumer driving-assistance features. I developed the questions and guide, recruited and interviewed participants, analyzed the accounts and wrote the final paper. This guide connects the skills I developed to the completed study and its materials.

## 1. Frame a question that interviews can investigate

I learned to turn a broad interest in human–AI interaction into questions about experience, expectations and interpretation. The initial protocol focused on communication modes and transitions of control. The final analysis connected those questions to perceived competence, negotiated control and communication in context.

**Applied in:** the [study protocol](../documents/interview-protocol.pdf), revised proposal and final paper.

**What I take forward:** choose a method for the kind of knowledge the team needs. Interviews can investigate why a person experiences an interaction in a particular way. A task study would answer a different question about observed interaction performance.

## 2. Plan recruitment and a manageable study

I practiced defining eligibility, explaining participation and choosing a feasible sampling approach. I recruited two adult drivers through convenience sampling and used approximately 30-minute Zoom interviews. The introduction covered voluntary participation, skipping questions and confidentiality.

**Applied in:** the protocol's recruitment plan and interview introduction.

**What I take forward:** make the recruitment rationale and sample scope explicit. These accounts provide depth about two experienced users; they do not estimate how common a preference is across drivers.

## 3. Interview with a consistent guide and responsive probes

I learned to use semi-structured questions to cover experience, communication, trust, control and accessibility while following the participant's account. Asking about a remembered situation helps ground a discussion. Follow-up questions about why something felt understandable, uncomfortable or reassuring can reveal the reasoning behind a stated preference.

**Applied in:** the interview guide and two completed interviews. Recordings were transcribed and checked for accuracy.

**What I take forward:** maintain consistent research topics while adapting the conversation. Distinguish a remembered event from an imagined scenario when interpreting the response.

## 4. Move from codes to an interpretation

I practiced reading transcripts, generating initial codes close to participants' language, refining codes and comparing patterns across the interviews. The paper includes a codebook with eight codes and develops three themes through an inductive reflexive thematic approach informed by Braun and Clarke.

**Applied in:** the final paper's method, codebook and findings; the [analysis process guide](analysis-process.md).

**What I take forward:** a theme should explain a pattern of meaning. A topic such as an alert identifies what was discussed; an interpretation explains how that communication relates to confidence, attention or control. The reader needs to see the relationship between the account and the analytic claim.

## 5. Examine my perspective through reflexivity

I learned to consider how my human-centered computing and accessibility background, and my positive expectations about automation, could shape my research questions and interpretation. The final paper makes that perspective available for examination.

**Applied in:** the paper's researcher positionality discussion and the course's reflexivity work.

**What I take forward:** reflexivity is an ongoing part of research reasoning. It helps me examine assumptions; it does not eliminate subjectivity or make one interpretation the only possible reading.

## 6. Translate findings into questions for design and engineering

The themes suggest interface questions about explaining system capabilities, making control options understandable and communicating in a way that fits the context. I learned to separate the participant's account, my interpretation and a proposed next design direction.

**Applied in:** the final discussion and the [theme interpretation guide](theme-interpretation.md).

**Example:** the communication theme motivates comparing routine-status and urgent-alert concepts. A subsequent prototype study could examine whether users understand the message and the available action. The interviews themselves did not test those prototypes or measure a safe handover interval.

**What I take forward:** give an interface team a clear question to evaluate, the evidence that motivated it and an account of what remains uncertain.

## 7. Communicate the method and the limits of a finding

I practiced writing a research account that connects the question, sampling, procedure, interpretation, researcher perspective and study scope. I learned to explain why a finding is useful while being specific about the evidence behind it.

**Applied in:** the [original final paper](../documents/UGBOKO_Final-Paper.pdf), preserved as supplied, and the portfolio case study.

**What I take forward:** communicate an exploratory insight without turning it into a claim about all users. Broader recruitment and scenario-based observation would help extend the work. The study did not measure driving performance or validate a production interface.
